12. What Is the Turing Test?

The Turing Test is an idea introduced by Alan Turing in 1950. It considers whether a machine can communicate in such a way that a human evaluator cannot reliably distinguish its responses from those of a human.

Simple Explanation: A person communicates with a human and a machine without knowing which is which. If the person cannot reliably tell them apart through conversation, the machine may be considered to have performed well in the test.

Real-Life Example: Imagine chatting through text with two hidden participants. One is a person and the other is an AI system. You try to identify which participant is the computer.

Why It Matters: The test became an influential way to discuss machine intelligence, although it does not measure every aspect of intelligence.

18. What Is Symbolic Artificial Intelligence?

Symbolic AI is an approach to artificial intelligence that represents information using symbols, facts, rules, and logical relationships. A computer program uses these representations to reason and solve problems.

Simple Explanation: Instead of learning everything from large collections of examples, a symbolic AI system follows rules and knowledge provided to it.

Real-Life Example: A simple expert system might use rules such as: if a machine is overheating and its cooling system has failed, recommend checking the cooling system.

Why It Matters: Symbolic AI played a major role in early AI research and remains useful in areas where rules and explanations are important.

Related Questions: What is machine learning? How is symbolic AI different from modern AI?

19. What Was ELIZA and Why Was It Important?

ELIZA was an early computer program created by Joseph Weizenbaum at MIT in the 1960s. It simulated conversation by recognizing patterns in a user's text and producing responses based on predefined rules. One of its well-known scripts imitated a conversational therapist.

Simple Explanation: ELIZA was a program that could respond to typed messages in a way that sometimes appeared conversational, even though it did not understand language like a human.

Real-Life Example: If someone typed, "I feel worried," ELIZA might respond with a question such as, "Why do you feel worried?"

Why It Matters: ELIZA demonstrated how simple language patterns could create the impression of conversation and helped researchers explore human-computer interaction.

Related Questions: How did early chatbots work? How are modern AI chatbots different from ELIZA?

Real-Life Example: An early program might solve a particular logic puzzle using predefined rules. A modern machine-learning system can be trained on many examples to recognize patterns in images or text.

Why It Matters: Understanding this difference helps explain how AI developed from small experimental programs into the wide range of technologies used today.

23. What Was the General Problem Solver?

Answer

The General Problem Solver, commonly called GPS, was an early AI program developed by Allen Newell, Herbert A. Simon, and their collaborators in the late 1950s. It was designed to solve problems by representing goals, identifying differences between the current situation and the desired result, and applying suitable operations.

Simple Explanation

GPS attempted to solve problems by breaking them into smaller steps and working toward a specific goal.

Real-Life Example

Imagine planning a journey from one city to another. A problem-solving system can consider the starting point, destination, and possible steps needed to reach the goal.

Why It Matters

The first AI winter was a time when expectations about AI were higher than what the technology could realistically deliver. As a result, financial support and public excitement decreased.

34. What Is an Inference Engine in AI?

Answer

An inference engine is a component of a knowledge-based AI system that applies logical rules to available facts to produce conclusions. It determines which rules are relevant and how they should be applied.

Simple Explanation

Why It Matters

35. What Is Forward Chaining in Artificial Intelligence?

Answer

Forward chaining is a reasoning method that starts with known facts and applies rules step by step to discover new facts or reach a conclusion. It is commonly associated with rule-based expert systems.

Simple Explanation

Forward chaining moves from available information toward a possible conclusion.

Real-Life Example

A simple educational system might know that a student completed all required lessons and passed the final assessment. It can apply its rules to determine whether the student qualifies for a course certificate.

Why It Matters

This method allows rule-based systems to discover conclusions from information that is already available.

36. What Is Backward Chaining in Artificial Intelligence?

Answer

Backward chaining is a reasoning method that begins with a specific goal or conclusion and works backward to determine whether the available facts and rules support it.

Simple Explanation

Instead of starting with all known facts, backward chaining starts with a question and checks what information is needed to answer it.

Real-Life Example

A troubleshooting system may begin with the question, "Is the internet connection working?" It then checks relevant conditions, such as network status and device connectivity.

Why It Matters

Backward chaining is useful when a system needs to test a particular possibility rather than explore every available fact.
